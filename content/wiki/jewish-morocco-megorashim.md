# Jewish Morocco — The *Megorashim*

1492 – present
The Sephardic exile community

## What It Was

*Megorashim* — "the expelled ones" — is the Hebrew term the post-1492 Iberian Jewish refugees used for themselves, to mark the rupture and to distinguish themselves from the [[jewish-morocco-toshavim]], the indigenous Moroccan Jews already living in the country.

The first wave came after the Alhambra Decree of March 1492, which gave the Jews of Castile and Aragon four months to convert or leave. Tens of thousands crossed the Strait of Gibraltar; the lucky ones reached Fes, Tetouan, Salé, Larache. The Wattasid sultans permitted entry but the conditions were brutal — famine, plague, robbery on the road, a fire that destroyed the Jewish quarter of Fes in 1494. Many died, many returned, many continued east to the Ottoman lands. Those who stayed were joined by a second wave after 1497, when Manuel I of Portugal forcibly converted the Jews who had fled there from Spain; over the following century, *conversos* and their descendants slipped back to Judaism by emigrating to Morocco.

By 1550 the megorashim had reorganized northern Moroccan Jewry around themselves. They brought Castilian Spanish (which, frozen in Morocco, became the Judeo-Spanish dialect [[haketia-and-judeo-arabic|Haketía]]); they brought Sephardic liturgy, which displaced the older toshavim rite in most of the cities they settled; they brought commercial networks reaching from Livorno to Amsterdam to the Caribbean; and they brought a class of court Jews — *negociadores del rey* — who served Saadian and Alaouite sultans as diplomats, treasurers, and trans-Mediterranean fixers. Tetouan became, effectively, an Iberian Jewish city in exile. Mogador (Essaouira), founded in 1764 by Sultan Sidi Mohammed ben Abdellah, was designed around megorashim merchants — the *tujjar al-sultan*, the sultan's merchants — to handle European trade.

For three centuries the two communities coexisted in the same cities without merging: separate synagogues, separate rabbinical courts, separate cemeteries, separate marriage circles. By the nineteenth century the distinction had softened — intermarriage, shared schools, the Alliance Israélite Universelle from 1862 — but the labels persisted into living memory.

## Why It Matters

The megorashim are how Andalusi Spain enters Morocco a second time. The first entry was the Muslim *andalusi* refugees of 1492 and the morisco expulsions of 1609, who shaped Tetouan, Chefchaouen, Salé and Fes — see [[andalusi-inheritance]]. The second entry, in parallel, is Jewish: same cities, same Iberian melancholy, same cuisine adapted, same architecture transposed. Northern Morocco's distinctive feel — the Andalusian tile, the patio house, the slow vowels, the flamenco-adjacent melodies — is the joint product of both expulsions.

The megorashim also gave Moroccan Jewry its modern face. By the time of the French Protectorate in 1912, the megorashim-descended urban elite — Tangier, Tetouan, Casablanca, Fes — were the bridge between Morocco and Europe: French and Spanish-speaking, internationally networked, the first Moroccans into European-style banking, journalism, medicine and law. They produced a disproportionate share of the country's twentieth-century intellectual and commercial class — and then, between 1948 and the early 1970s, almost all of them left, for Israel, France, Spain, Venezuela, and Canada.

## What Survives

Tetouan: the *Juderia*, intact in plan if not in population, with the Rabbi Isaac Bengualid Synagogue restored as a museum. Fes: the original *mellah* of 1438, several restored synagogues (Ibn Danan, Slat al-Fassiyine), and the Jewish cemetery on the slope below the royal palace. Mogador / Essaouira: the densest concentration of preserved Jewish architecture in Morocco — the *Bayt Dakira* memorial museum, the Slat Lkahal synagogue, dozens of houses with mezuzah niches still in their doorframes. Casablanca: the only functioning Jewish community of any size left in the country, plus the Museum of Moroccan Judaism in the Oasis quarter — the only Jewish museum in the Arab world.

The Haketía dialect survives in pockets in northern Morocco and more vigorously in the Tetouani diaspora in Caracas, Buenos Aires, and Madrid. Sephardic-Moroccan cuisine — *adafina*, *pastela*, *dafina*-style Saturday stews — is now part of the broader Moroccan kitchen, often without any memory that it travelled from Toledo via Tetouan.
