# Al-Andalus and the Maghreb

The Strait of Gibraltar is fourteen kilometres wide. For nine centuries it functioned less as a border than as a hinge: a single Arabo-Berber-Andalusi cultural world spread on both sides of it, with people, troops, scholars, refugees, and dynasties moving back and forth. The point of this entry is to thread that connection chronologically — because while [[al-andalus]] tells the Iberian half and the Moroccan dynastic timelines tell the African half, the cross-strait flow itself is the headline.

For the Iberian backbone see [[al-andalus]]. For the Moroccan dynastic side see [[the-almoravids]], [[the-almohads]], [[the-marinids]]. For the architectural through-line see [[almohad-architecture]].

## The Hinge

Four places on each side did most of the work. On the African shore: **Tangier** (Tanja, Roman Tingis), **Ceuta** (Sebta, Roman Septem), and the inland gateway through **Fez**. On the Iberian shore: **Tarifa** (named for Tarif ibn Malik), **Algeciras** (al-Jazīra al-Khaḍrā', 'the Green Island'), **Gibraltar** (Jabal Tariq), and through the mountains, **Córdoba** — then **Seville** — then **Granada**. The pivot moved south on the Andalusi side as the Reconquista pressed; the African anchor moved from Kairouan (8th c.) to Fez (9th c.) to Marrakesh (11th–13th c.) to Fez again (Marinid).

## Six Crossings That Mattered

### 1. The Conquest (710 – 712)

- **710** — Tarif ibn Malik makes a reconnaissance raid from Tangier with c. 400 men. Lands at Tarifa.
- **711** — [[tariq-ibn-ziyad|Tariq ibn Ziyad]], an Amazigh governor of Tangier under the Umayyad governor of Ifriqiya, crosses with c. 7,000 mostly Berber troops. Lands at Jabal Tariq (Gibraltar). Defeats King Roderic at Guadalete (July 711). Takes Toledo by year's end.
- **712** — The Umayyad governor of Ifriqiya, Musa ibn Nusayr, arrives with c. 18,000 Arab troops, jealous of Tariq's success. The two campaign together until Caliph al-Walid recalls them both to Damascus.

The demographic fact: the conquest of Iberia is in the first instance an Amazigh demographic event, not an Arab one. The garrison towns of al-Andalus were settled with Berber troops. The 740 revolt is consequently a Berber revolt against Arab tax discrimination, with consequences on both sides of the strait simultaneously.

### 2. The Umayyad Restoration (756) and the Cordoban Sphere

- **756** — **Abd al-Rahman I** — the surviving Umayyad fleeing the Abbasid massacre — lands in al-Andalus from North Africa. His mother was a Nafza Berber from the Maghrib al-Aqsa; his five years of exile (750–755) were spent under his maternal uncles' protection. He proclaims himself emir at Córdoba.
- **818 [The rabadī exile]** — The southern suburb of Córdoba revolts; al-Hakam I crushes it and exiles the entire population. Many sail for the eastern Mediterranean and end up taking Crete from Byzantium (824). A second wave settles in **Fez**, in the *'Adwat al-Andalus* (the Andalusi quarter), and builds the **Andalusi Mosque** (859). Fez's defining double-medina structure dates from this moment.
- **9th–10th c.** — The Cordoban caliphate exerts deep cultural influence on the Maghreb. Maliki jurisprudence, the Cordoban architectural school (carved plaster, polylobed arches, T-plan mosques), and Andalusi musical and culinary traditions all spread west.

### 3. The Almoravid Intervention (1086 – 1147)

- **1085** — Toledo falls to Alfonso VI. The Abbadid emir of Seville, al-Mu'tamid, writes to **Yusuf ibn Tashfin** at Marrakesh: 'Better to herd camels in the Sahara than to herd swine in Castile.'
- **1086 [Sagrajas / Zallāqa]** — Yusuf crosses from Ceuta to Algeciras with a Saharan Sanhaja army. Destroys Alfonso VI at Sagrajas (Zallāqa), 23 October 1086.
- **1090–1094** — Yusuf returns and deposes the taifa kings one after another. Al-Andalus is annexed to the Almoravid empire — ruled from Marrakesh.
- **1086–1147** — Continuous flow of Andalusi craftsmen, jurists, and architects to the Maghreb. The Almoravid mosque of Tlemcen (1136) and the *minbar* of the Koutoubia (made in Córdoba 1137) date from this period — Andalusi work for Maghrebi patrons.

### 4. The Almohad Conquest (1146 – 1212)

- **1146–1147** — [[the-almohads|Abd al-Mu'min]] crosses to take al-Andalus from the collapsing Almoravid order. Seville falls 1147. The Almohads make Seville their Andalusi capital.
- **1172–1199** — Major construction in Seville: the **Great Mosque** (1172–1198), the **Giralda** (1184–1198), the Almohad core of the **Alcázar**. The architects shuttle between Marrakesh, Rabat, and Seville. The Almohad architectural school is one continuous practice on both sides of the strait — see [[almohad-architecture]].
- **1195 [Alarcos]** — Yacoub al-Mansour's victory over Castile. Commemorated by the foundation of the **Hassan complex** in Rabat — al-Andalus politically reshapes Morocco.
- **1212 [Las Navas de Tolosa]** — Catastrophic Almohad defeat. The dynastic gravity shifts back to Marrakesh; the Andalusi end of the empire begins to peel away.

### 5. The Marinid Expeditions (1275 – 1340)

- **1275–1340** — Six Marinid expeditions from Fez and Tangier, in support of the Nasrids of Granada and against Castile. Crossings via Tangier-Tarifa.
- **1340 [Río Salado]** — 30 October. Combined Castilian-Portuguese forces destroy the Marinid army at the Salado river near Tarifa. Sultan Abu al-Hasan barely escapes. **The last Maghrebi army to cross the strait** until the 20th century.

### 6. The Refugee Crossings (1492 and 1609)

- **1492 [The Granada exodus]** — After Boabdil's surrender on 2 January, tens of thousands emigrate. Not a single coordinated movement but a slow, long emigration over decades. Major destinations:
  - **Tetouan** — refounded after 1484 by **Sidi al-Mandri**, an Andalusi noble from Granada. The medina's street plan and house typology are pure Granadan transplants.
  - **Chefchaouen** — founded 1471 by Mulay Ali ibn Rashid as a base against the Portuguese; populated by Granadan refugees.
  - **Fez** — the Andalusi quarter, already eight hundred years old, swells again.
  - **Tlemcen** — the Zayyanid capital absorbs scholars and merchants.
  - **Tunis** — Hafsid receiving city; the surnames *al-Andalusi*, *al-Gharnati*, *al-Qurtubi* are still common.
- **1502–1526** — Forced conversions throughout Castile, then Aragon and Valencia. The remaining Iberian Muslims become **Moriscos**: nominally Christian, in many cases secretly Muslim.
- **1568–1571 [Alpujarras]** — Morisco rebellion in the Sierra Nevada. Crushed; Granadan Moriscos forcibly dispersed across Castile.
- **1609–1614 [The expulsion]** — Philip III decrees the expulsion of all Moriscos. **Roughly 300,000 people** — perhaps 4% of Spain's population — forced out in five years. Major Moroccan and North African destinations:
  - **Salé** — the **Hornacheros** (from the village of Hornachos in Extremadura) arrive in 1610 as a coherent armed group; they take the New Salé (Rabat side) and within a decade establish the **Salé Republic** (1624–1668), an Andalusi corsair statelet on the Bouregreg.
  - **Rabat** — The Andalusi Wall around the southern medina dates from this moment.
  - **Tetouan** — the second great refugee wave, after 1492.
  - **Fez** — the Andalusi quarter takes a third infusion.
  - **Tunis and the Medjerda valley** — the Hafsid (now Ottoman) authorities settle Moriscos in the rural Medjerda; **Testour**, **Soliman**, and **El Alia** are 17th-century Morisco-Andalusi foundations whose architecture and dialect still mark them out.

## Things You Can Still See

- **The Andalusi Quarter of Fez** ('Adwat al-Andalus) — the eastern half of Fes el-Bali. The Andalusi Mosque (859) anchors it.
- **Tetouan medina** — the most thoroughly Andalusi urban survival anywhere in Morocco. UNESCO World Heritage 1997.
- **Chefchaouen medina** — the blue-washed houses are a 20th-century intervention; the Andalusi-Granadan plan and the Spanish-laced Arabic of the medina are not.
- **The Andalusi Wall, Rabat** — the southern medina's circuit, 17th-century Morisco construction.
- **Testour, Tunisia** — the Great Mosque of Testour has a Spanish-style bell tower (with no bell), built by 17th-century Morisco refugees who couldn't quite shake the Castilian visual reflex.
- **The Giralda, Seville** — the most visited surviving fragment of Maghrebi architecture in Iberia. See [[almohad-architecture]].

## Threads Worth Following

- **The Sephardic parallel.** The 1492 Alhambra Decree expelled both Muslim and Jewish populations; many Sephardim took the same routes to the same Moroccan and Tunisian cities, founding parallel Sephardic communities (*megorashim*) in Tetouan, Salé, Fez, and Tunis. → [[jewish-morocco-toshavim]]
- **The Salafi-Andalusi reformist current.** Some of the most influential 19th- and early 20th-century Moroccan reformers — Allal al-Fasi, the Fasi-Andalusi families of Fez — traced their lineage to 1492 and 1609 refugees. The political idea of Morocco as the Andalusi heir-state has long roots.
- **The corsair economy.** The Salé Republic and the related Tetouan corsairs are direct outcomes of the expulsions — a Morisco economic strategy for using the Atlantic to do back to Spain what Spain had done to them.
