# Romans in Morocco

Rome ruled the northern third of modern Morocco for roughly four and a half centuries — from the annexation of Mauretania in 40 CE to Diocletian's withdrawal around 285 CE — and held a coastal toehold for another three after that, until the Arab conquest of the early 8th century. The province was *Mauretania Tingitana*. Its capital was Tingis, modern Tangier. Its largest city was Volubilis, near Meknes.

The distinctive thing about Roman Morocco is how shallow it was. Rome never extended formal authority south of a line running from Sala (Rabat) through Volubilis to Tamuda (Tetouan). Beyond that line lay the *Mauri* — the Baquates, Bavares, Quinquegentanei — confederations the empire managed by treaty rather than rule. The relationship is recorded on a series of inscribed altars set up at Volubilis between 173 and 280 CE, on which Roman procurators and Baquates chiefs renew their *colloquium* (formal conversation). Pacification by handshake, not by garrison.

## Before the Province (c. 200 BCE – 40 CE)

- **3rd c. BCE** — Mauretanian kingdom under Bocchus I and his successors. Independent Amazigh polity in close trade contact with Carthage and, after 146 BCE, with Rome.
- **105 BCE** — Bocchus I betrays his son-in-law Jugurtha (king of Numidia) to Sulla, ending the Jugurthine War. The arrangement is commemorated in a frieze on the Capitoline Hill in Rome — the first major Roman engagement with Mauretania, and entirely diplomatic.
- **33 BCE** — Bocchus III dies and bequeaths the kingdom to Octavian. Augustus does not annex; he installs veteran colonies (Zilil, Babba, Banasa) in 33 BCE and keeps the kingdom in the imperial orbit as a buffer.
- **25 BCE** — Augustus restores a Mauretanian client kingdom under [[juba-ii]] — Numidian by birth, raised in Rome, married to Cleopatra Selene II (daughter of Cleopatra VII and Mark Antony). Juba rules from a double capital: Caesarea (Iol, modern Cherchell) on the eastern coast, with Volubilis as the western seat. He is one of the most learned monarchs of his age — wrote books on Africa, Arabia, and Libyan herbs that Pliny still cited a century later.
- **23 CE** — Juba II dies. His son Ptolemy succeeds; rules quietly and well.
- **40 CE** — Caligula summons Ptolemy to Rome and has him assassinated. Reasons disputed; Suetonius blames imperial jealousy of Ptolemy's purple cloak at the games.

## The Province of Mauretania Tingitana (40 – c. 285 CE)

- **40–44 CE [Aedemon's Revolt]** — Aedemon, a freedman of the murdered Ptolemy, leads a Mauretanian revolt that takes four years and the legions of Suetonius Paulinus (the future conqueror of Boudicca) to suppress. The campaigns push briefly south of the Atlas — the deepest Roman penetration of Morocco ever recorded.
- **44 CE [Province created]** — Claudius divides Juba's kingdom into two provinces. Mauretania Tingitana (capital: Tingis) takes the western half, modern northern Morocco; Mauretania Caesariensis (capital: Iol Caesarea) takes the eastern half, central and western Algeria. Tingitana is administratively attached to the Diocese of Hispania across the strait — Roman Morocco is governed from Iberia, not from the African continent.
- **late 1st c. CE** — Roman urban network consolidated. *Coloniae* at Lixus, Banasa, Zilil, Babba; *municipium* status for Volubilis, which becomes the southern administrative center. The road network ties them along the Atlantic coast.
- **2nd c. CE** — Volubilis at peak. Triumphal arch of Caracalla erected 217 CE. Olive oil exported north. Garrison strength of one legionary detachment plus auxiliaries; the *Cohors I Asturum* and others are attested.
- **168–175 CE [Mauri raids]** — Major Mauri raids cross from Africa into Baetica (southern Iberia) under Marcus Aurelius. The empire responds with the Volubilis altars: a renewed treaty regime with the Baquates that holds, in periodically renegotiated form, for the next century.
- **193–211 CE [Severans]** — Septimius Severus (himself a Lepcis Magna African) and Caracalla pour resources into the African provinces. Volubilis gets its arch in 217.
- **253–268 CE [Crisis]** — The Crisis of the Third Century reaches Tingitana. Frontier inscriptions thin out. Garrison strength reduced.

## The Withdrawal (c. 285 – 429 CE)

- **c. 285 CE** — Diocletian's reorganization. Roman administration withdraws south of the Loukos river. Volubilis, Banasa, Sala, Thamusida, and Tocolosida are officially abandoned. The *limes Tingitanus* is pulled back to a strip from Tingis south to Lixus, east through Tamuda to the coast.
- **c. 297 CE** — Late Roman administrative reorganization formalizes the new shape. Tingitana is now a small province of the Diocese of Hispania, not of the Diocese of Africa.
- **4th c. CE** — Christianity established in the surviving cities. Latin Christian community at Volubilis (now outside the empire) continues; their tombstones run into the 7th century, the longest documented post-Roman survival in the Maghreb.

## Vandals, Byzantines, Visigoths (429 – c. 711)

- **429 CE [Vandals]** — Genseric crosses from Spain at Tingis with c. 80,000 Vandals and Alans, marches east to Carthage (which falls 439). Tingitana is briefly under Vandal authority but is not a Vandal core territory; effective control reverts to local Mauri-Roman elites.
- **533 CE [Byzantine reconquest]** — Belisarius recovers Vandal Africa for Justinian. Byzantine authority in northwestern Morocco amounts to the garrison at Septem (Ceuta), perhaps Tingis briefly, and a few coastal points.
- **late 6th c.** — Septem passes to the Visigoths of Spain.
- **c. 711 CE [Arab conquest]** — Musa ibn Nusayr's lieutenant Tariq ibn Ziyad takes Tingis and crosses to Iberia. End of the Roman → Byzantine → Visigothic continuum on the African shore of the strait.

## Sites Worth Visiting

- **Volubilis** (UNESCO 1997) — the canonical Roman site of Morocco. Triumphal arch, basilica, capitol, and the great mosaic floors of the House of Orpheus and the House of Venus.
- **Lixus** — fortified hilltop above Larache. Garum tanks, basilica, mosaics. Far less visited than Volubilis.
- **Sala / Chellah** (Rabat) — a Roman colonia inside what became a 14th-century Marinid royal necropolis. Stork-topped minaret over Roman foundations.
- **Banasa** — quiet ruin on the Sebou. The chief draw is the historiographic record (the Baquates inscriptions are largely from here and from Volubilis).
- **Cotta** — garum and purple-dye factory ruins on the cliffs west of Tangier near Cap Spartel.

## Threads Worth Following

- **The Amazigh substrate.** Mauretania Tingitana is laid over a Mauri tribal landscape that long predates Rome and outlasts it. → [[amazigh-timeline]], [[amazigh-substrate]]
- **The Hispania connection.** Roman Morocco is administered from Spain across the strait — a geographic logic the Visigoths, the Almoravids, the Marinids, and Spanish Morocco all rediscover. → [[strait-of-gibraltar]]
