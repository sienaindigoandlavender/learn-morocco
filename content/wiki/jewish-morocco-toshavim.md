# Jewish Morocco — The *Toshavim*

Antiquity – 1492
Native, pre-Sephardic Jewish community

## What It Was

*Toshavim* — literally "residents," "the ones already here" — is the term the Sephardic newcomers used after 1492 to distinguish the older Jewish community from themselves. The toshavim were the indigenous Jews of the Maghreb, by then already two millennia deep into the country.

The earliest plausible arrivals are Phoenician-Punic, the merchant Jews who came west with Carthage; the next layer is Roman, after the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE and the Bar Kokhba revolt in 135 CE, when Jewish refugees and slaves were dispersed across the western Mediterranean. By late antiquity there were Jewish communities in Volubilis (inscriptions), in the Sous, in the Drâa Valley, and across the Atlas. Many of them spoke Berber — *Tamazight*-speaking Jews are documented in Moroccan sources well into the twentieth century — and several medieval Arab chroniclers (notably Ibn Khaldun) report that whole Berber tribes were Jewish or Judaized before the Arab conquest, including the Jarawa of the Aurès whose semi-legendary leader, [[al-kahina]], resisted the Arab armies in the 690s.

After the Islamic conquest the community was reorganized under [[dhimmi-status]] — protected, taxed, restricted — and integrated into the new urban economy. Under the Idrisids and the Berber empires the toshavim were central to the trans-Saharan trade in gold, salt, and slaves; the southern oasis towns of Sijilmasa, Tamegroute, and Akka were partly Jewish entrepôts. They prayed in their own rite (the *minhag al-toshavim*, closer to Babylonian than Iberian usage), spoke Judeo-Berber and Judeo-Arabic, and lived inside the cities rather than in walled quarters — until the founding of the first [[mellah]] in Fes in 1438, a century before the Sephardic wave arrived to fill it.

## Why It Matters

The toshavim are the part of Moroccan Jewish history that most popular accounts skip. The Sephardic story — Maimonides in Fes, the Inquisition, the exile, Tangier and Tetouan as Spanish-Jewish cities — is well-known. The two-thousand-year-deep Berber-Jewish substrate underneath it is not. Foregrounding the toshavim does two things: it makes Moroccan Jewishness older than Moroccan Arabness, and it makes it Amazigh as well as Sephardic. It dissolves the easy reading of "Arab Muslim country with a Jewish minority" into something stranger and truer.

The toshavim also explain the geographical distribution of Moroccan Jewry. The Sephardic exiles after 1492 went to the northern and Atlantic cities. But Jewish communities in the High Atlas, the Anti-Atlas, the Drâa, the Sous, and the desert oases — the Jews who pilgrimage sites in the south commemorate, the Jews of the Berber dialects — those are toshavim. The further you go from the Mediterranean coast, the older the community.

## What Survives

In Morocco itself, very little of the live community remains: emigration after 1948 and 1967 reduced the rural toshavim communities to nothing. What survives is material and toponymic. Ruined synagogues in Akka and the Drâa. Saint-tombs (*tsadikim*) in the High Atlas, still pilgrimage sites for the Moroccan diaspora. Berber-Jewish music recorded in the 1950s and 1960s and now reissued. Place-names with *mellah* and *yahud* roots. The Bahia Palace in Marrakesh, built by a vizier whose financial backbone was Jewish-toshavim merchants from the south.

In the diaspora — Israel, France, Montreal — the toshavim survive as a distinct subgroup of *Mizrahim*, with their own liturgical melodies, their own piyyutim, their own saint cults, and an increasingly active heritage scene that distinguishes them from their Sephardic cousins.
