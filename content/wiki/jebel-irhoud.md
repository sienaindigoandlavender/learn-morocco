# Jebel Irhoud

c. 300,000 BP
Site / archaeological discovery

## What It Is

A former barite mine on a hillside about 100 km west of Marrakesh, between the Atlantic plain and the western High Atlas. In the 1960s miners cutting into the hill exposed a fossil-bearing cave fill; in 2017 a team led by Jean-Jacques Hublin (Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology) published a redating and reinterpretation of the bones found there. The verdict: the skull, jaw and limb fragments of at least five individuals, together with Middle Stone Age tools and burnt flint, are roughly 300,000 years old — the oldest fossils anywhere on Earth that can reasonably be called *Homo sapiens*.

## Why It Matters

Before Jebel Irhoud, the consensus origin date for our species was around 200,000 years ago, in East Africa — Omo Kibish and Herto in Ethiopia. Irhoud pushed that back by roughly 100,000 years and dragged the geography westward across the continent. The current reading is not "humans came from Morocco" but something subtler and stranger: early *Homo sapiens* was already a pan-African species 300,000 years ago, with populations spread from the Maghreb to the Horn, evolving in a network rather than at a single point. Morocco sits at one end of that network, and at present holds the earliest dated specimens.

The Irhoud faces are also transitional: the lower face is essentially modern, but the braincase is still long and low, more archaic. They are the clearest physical record we have of a species in the process of becoming itself.

## What Survives

The fossils themselves are at the Institut National des Sciences de l'Archéologie et du Patrimoine (INSAP) in Rabat. The site is not currently open to casual visitors — it is an active research locus, fenced and protected. The wider story enters the country indirectly: the Rabat Archaeological Museum and a handful of regional museums display casts and contextual material, and Irhoud is now a fixture of every popular account of human origins.
