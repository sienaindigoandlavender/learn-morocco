# Almohad Architecture

For about half a century — from the founding of Tin Mal in the late 1140s to the abandonment of the Hassan project in 1199 — a single architectural school built across an empire that ran from Lisbon to Tripoli. The Almohads inherited the Almoravid building tradition, made it monumental, and standardized it: the square minaret with a small lantern above; the brick sebka net; the sober interior of horseshoe arches; the ceremonial gate as a triumphal arch in masonry. The dynasty fell in 1269. The architectural language outlived it by centuries — the Marinids, the Hafsids, the Nasrids of Granada, the Mudéjar masons of Christian Castile all worked in dialects of it.

## The Almohad Architectural Language

Four features make a building recognisably Almohad. They are all already implicit in late Almoravid work; the Almohads simply built them at imperial scale and held to them across two thousand kilometres of empire.

1. **The square-shaft minaret with a small lantern.** Stone or brick, no taper, terminating in a smaller square upper section topped by a *yamur* (gilded-copper finial of stacked spheres). Set the model that Koutoubia, Giralda, Hassan, and the Kasbah Mosque of Marrakesh all repeat at different scales — 12.7-metre, 13.6-metre, 13-metre, and 8-metre square bases respectively, varying in height but identical in geometry.
2. **The sebka net.** A diamond lattice in cut brick or stucco, originating in Almoravid carved-plaster work but here translated into a heavy facing material that runs over whole walls. Most spectacular on the upper shafts of the Giralda and Kasbah Mosque minarets.
3. **The T-plan hypostyle mosque.** Wide central nave perpendicular to the qibla, intersected by a transverse aisle along the qibla wall. Five-bay or seven-bay variants. Direct descent from the Cordoban model via the Almoravid mosque of Tlemcen, but cleaner in plan.
4. **The triumphal gate.** Bent-axis defensive entry on the inside, but the outer face is a frontispiece: a horseshoe (or polylobed) arch under a rectangular alfiz, framed by foliate Kufic inscription bands and carved spandrels. Bab Agnaou, Bab Oudaia, and Bab er-Rouah are the masterworks.

What the Almohads did *not* do, on doctrinal grounds, was the figurative ornament or the coloured marble veneer of the Cordoban tradition. Almohad surfaces are sober: brick, carved plaster, an occasional band of green-and-white faience. The aesthetic was a programme — a visible repudiation of late-Almoravid "luxury" the way the Almohads understood it.

## Timeline

### Origins (1121 – 1147)

- **1121** — Ibn Tumart proclaims himself Mahdi at Tinmel in the High Atlas. The Almohad movement is theological before it is architectural.
- **1130** — Battle of al-Buhayra (defeat) at Marrakesh; Ibn Tumart dies; Abd al-Mu'min, his Zenata lieutenant, succeeds and begins the long campaign against the Almoravids.
- **1147** — Abd al-Mu'min takes Marrakesh. The first **Koutoubia** is begun immediately on the site of the Almoravid Dar al-Hajar palace.

### The Dynastic Building Programme (1148 – 1199)

- **1148–1156 [Tin Mal]** — Abd al-Mu'min builds the **Tin Mal Mosque** in the Atlas as Ibn Tumart's mausoleum. Square minaret directly above the mihrab — the most unusual placement in Almohad architecture, not repeated.
- **1158 [Koutoubia II]** — The first Koutoubia is found to be misaligned with the qibla by about five degrees. The second mosque is begun adjacent to the first; the two operate together for some decades. The minaret rises slowly, completed only c. 1195 under Yacoub al-Mansour.
- **1159 [Ifriqiya]** — Abd al-Mu'min conquers Mahdiyya and Tunis. The **Kasbah of Tunis** is founded as the seat of Almohad authority in Ifriqiya — the only major Almohad foundation east of Algiers.
- **1163 [Succession]** — Abd al-Mu'min dies; **Abu Yaqub Yusuf** (r. 1163–1184) succeeds. Major construction shifts toward Seville and the Andalusian capital project.
- **1170s** — Reconstruction of the **Alcázar of Seville** begins under Abu Yaqub Yusuf.
- **1172** — The **Great Mosque of Seville** is begun. Architects Ahmad ibn Baso (initial) and Ali al-Ghumari (later phases). The largest mosque ever built in al-Andalus.
- **1184** — Abu Yaqub Yusuf dies at the siege of Santarém; **Yacoub al-Mansour** (r. 1184–1199) succeeds. Construction begins on the **Giralda**, minaret of the Seville mosque.
- **1185–1190 [Marrakesh]** — Yacoub al-Mansour founds the new royal city south of the Marrakesh medina. The **Kasbah Mosque** is built at its centre; **Bab Agnaou** is its ceremonial gate.
- **1188** — Inauguration of the mihrab of the Seville Great Mosque.
- **1195 [Alarcos]** — Almohad victory over Castile at Alarcos. Yacoub al-Mansour adopts the title *al-Mansur Billah* and inaugurates the largest building project of the dynasty: the new imperial city at **Ribat al-Fath** (modern Rabat), with a great mosque whose minaret — the **Hassan Tower** — is intended to surpass anything yet built. Construction begins immediately. The **Bab Oudaia** is the ceremonial gate of the kasbah.
- **1197 [Bab er-Rouah]** — Built into the western wall of Rabat. The matched companion to Bab Oudaia.
- **1198** — The Giralda's *yamur* is raised. The Seville mosque is functionally complete.
- **1199** — **Yacoub al-Mansour dies.** Construction of the Hassan complex stops permanently. The minaret stands at 44 of an intended 60 metres; the prayer hall reaches roof level on its outer walls but the columns inside remain bare. Both stand as ruins to this day.

### Late and Posthumous (1199 – 1269)

- **1212 [Las Navas de Tolosa]** — Catastrophic Almohad defeat in Iberia. The dynastic gravity shifts back to Marrakesh. Major imperial building effectively ends.
- **1220–1221 [Torre del Oro]** — The Almohad governor of Seville builds the **Torre del Oro** as a river-defence tower at the south end of the city. The latest important Almohad construction in al-Andalus.
- **1228–1229 [Hafsid secession]** — The Hafsid governor of Ifriqiya declares independence in Tunis; the Almohad architectural language passes intact into Hafsid practice.
- **1229 [Mallorca]** — James I of Aragon takes Mallorca. End of Almohad Balearics.
- **1236 [Córdoba]** — Falls to Castile. Almohad Calahorra Tower passes into Christian use; later (1369) substantially rebuilt.
- **1242 [Algarve]** — Silves and the Almohad Algarve fall to Portugal.
- **1248 [Seville]** — Falls to Ferdinand III of Castile. The Great Mosque becomes the cathedral; the Giralda becomes the bell tower. The Alcázar passes into royal Castilian use.
- **1269 [Marrakesh]** — Falls to the Marinids. End of the Almohad caliphate.

### Afterlives

- **1356–1366** — Pedro I of Castile commissions the **Mudéjar palace** at the Alcázar of Seville, in an idiom directly continuous with Almohad work — carved plaster, sebka, polylobed arches — executed in part by craftsmen lent by the Nasrid sultan of Granada.
- **1401** — The Great Mosque of Seville is demolished to begin the Gothic cathedral. The Patio de los Naranjos and the Giralda are kept.
- **1568** — **Hernán Ruiz II** completes the upper Renaissance bell-tower section of the Giralda — a four-storey campanile with the *giraldillo* weathervane, sitting directly on top of the Almohad shaft.
- **1755** — The Lisbon earthquake damages the Hassan Tower foundations and the Tin Mal mosque.
- **8 September 2023** — The Al Haouz earthquake severely damages the Tin Mal mosque. Restoration is now underway.

## Sites

### The Atlas Heartland

- **Tin Mal Mosque** — Atlas valley south of Marrakesh, near Talat n'Yaaqoub. Built 1148–1156 by Abd al-Mu'min as Ibn Tumart's mausoleum. Roofless ruin (now badly damaged), but the plan and the carved plaster qibla wall survive in striking condition. The *only* surviving Almohad mosque whose minaret rises above the mihrab itself.

### Marrakesh

- **Koutoubia Mosque** — The canonical Almohad great mosque. The 77-metre minaret is the model from which the Giralda and the Hassan Tower descend; the *yamur* is original. Note the foundations of the first Koutoubia (1147) immediately to the north of the second.
- **Kasbah Mosque** — Built 1185–1190 inside Yacoub al-Mansour's new royal city. The minaret is the most decorated of the Almohad towers — turquoise faience and a complex sebka brick net.
- **Bab Agnaou** — The ceremonial entrance to the kasbah. Carved blue-grey Guéliz stone from the Atlas. Concentric horseshoe arches, foliate Kufic frame, no figurative ornament.
- **Menara basin and gardens** — Almohad enlargement of an Almoravid hydraulic system feeding olive groves west of the medina. The pavilion above the basin is 19th-century Alaouite.

### Rabat (Ribat al-Fath)

- **Hassan Tower and Mosque** — The unfinished masterpiece. The plan was for the largest mosque of the western Islamic world; construction stopped at Yacoub al-Mansour's death in 1199. The minaret, 44 of 60 intended metres, and the rows of broken column shafts on the esplanade are all that remains. The Mohammed V Mausoleum (1971) sits at the qibla end.
- **Bab Oudaia** — The ceremonial gate of the Kasbah of the Udayas. Often regarded as the masterpiece of Almohad gate architecture — the proportional system of nested arches and the carved foliate frame are at their most refined here.
- **Bab er-Rouah** — The 'Gate of the Winds' on the western wall of Rabat. A matched companion piece to Bab Oudaia, built two years later.
- **Kasbah of the Udayas** — Almoravid foundation, refounded by Abd al-Mu'min as Ribat al-Fath, the staging citadel for the Andalus jihad. The current circuit walls and the small Almohad mosque inside date from this period.

### Seville and al-Andalus

- **Patio de los Naranjos and the Giralda** — What survives of the Great Mosque of Seville. The patio is the original courtyard; the Giralda is the minaret. The Almohad shaft rises 50 metres in brick-faced sebka work; the Renaissance bell-tower addition (1568) sits directly on top of it.
- **Torre del Oro** — Twelve-sided river-defence tower at the south end of Seville. Built 1220–1221 — the latest major Almohad construction in al-Andalus.
- **Alcázar of Seville** — The Almohad governor's palace. The Patio del Yeso and parts of the outer walls are visibly Almohad. Pedro I's 14th-century Mudéjar palace incorporates the Almohad plan and the Almohad idiom — the most direct line of architectural inheritance from Almohad to Christian Spain.
- **Calahorra Tower, Córdoba** — Almohad fortification at the south end of the Roman bridge across the Guadalquivir. Substantially rebuilt by Henry II of Castile in 1369.
- **Castle of Silves, Algarve** — Red-sandstone walls in distinctive Almohad masonry. Capital of the Algarve under the Almohads (Xelb in Arabic).
- **Walls of Niebla, Huelva** — Among the most complete Andalusi Islamic city walls anywhere. Forty towers, five gates.

### Ifriqiya

- **Kasbah of Tunis** — Founded by Abd al-Mu'min after the conquest of 1159 as the seat of Almohad authority in Ifriqiya. The visible Kasbah Mosque and most surviving structures are early Hafsid (after 1228), built in an idiom directly inherited from the Almohad western tradition. The cleanest example of how the Almohad architectural language outlived the Almohad state.

## Threads Worth Following

- **The Cordoban inheritance.** Almohad mosque plans descend from the 8th-century Cordoba mosque via the 11th-century Almoravid mosque of Tlemcen. The line is unbroken from Abd al-Rahman I to Yacoub al-Mansour.
- **The doctrinal sobriety.** The absence of figurative imagery and coloured marble veneer is not a poverty of means — it is a programme. Almohad surfaces are deliberately what late-Almoravid surfaces are not.
- **The Mudéjar afterlife.** The Castilian and Aragonese kings who took over Almohad cities did not destroy the buildings; in many cases (the Alcázar of Seville being the headline example) they hired the same craftsmen and continued the same idiom for another two centuries.
- **The Hafsid line.** Tunis after 1229 is the eastern continuation of the Almohad architectural school in a way that Marinid Morocco — with its more figurative, Andalusi-leaning ornament — is not.
